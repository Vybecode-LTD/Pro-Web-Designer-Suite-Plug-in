# Objection Handling

The live conversation. What the objection usually means underneath, the response that
works, and the response that loses the room.

The deck is rehearsal; this is the meeting. Every entry follows one shape, because the
shape is the skill: **what was said → what it usually means → what works → what loses.**

## 1. The catalogue

### 1.1 "I don't like the colour"

**Usually means:** one of four completely different things — it is unfamiliar; it is not
their brand; it is genuinely wrong for the market; or they said "colour" because it is the
only vocabulary they have for "something about this feels off to me".

**Works:** find out which. *"That's useful — is it that it doesn't feel like you, or that
it's not what you expected, or is something else about the page doing it?"* Then answer the
real objection. If it is a preference and the colour is parameterised, change it — it costs
minutes and buys you the next argument (§5).

**Loses:** defending the colour. You win on measurement, lose on relationship, and it
returns in every subsequent meeting because it was overruled rather than resolved.

### 1.2 "It feels empty"

**Usually means:** "I am used to a denser page and this looks unfinished", or, often, "I am
worried my boss will think we did not get much for the money".

**Works:** name the mechanism and the tradeoff. *"That's deliberate — the median visit is
forty-one seconds, so each screen makes one point. If we fill the space, everything competes
and nothing gets read. But tell me what you feel is missing, because if there is content
that should be there, that is a different problem and I want to know."* The second half is
not politeness: "empty" often genuinely means *content is missing*, which is a real finding.

**Loses:** "that's white space, it's intentional." Smug, treats a real discomfort as
ignorance, and answers none of the worry underneath.

### 1.3 "Can we make the logo bigger?"

**Usually means:** "I do not feel represented on this page", or, for a founder, "this
company is my life and it is small on the screen". Almost never a real opinion about
pixel dimensions.

**Works:** acknowledge, then redirect from size to prominence. *"It can be bigger — can I
show you why it is that size first? The first thing a visitor needs is what you do and when
you can do it; that is what produces a booking. The logo confirms who you are once they are
already reading. If it does not feel present enough, we can give it more room around it
rather than more size, which usually reads as more confident."* Then, if they still want it
bigger, make it bigger. It is their logo.

**Loses:** the lecture about brand recognition and the F-pattern. Everyone has heard it, and
it treats their attachment to their own company as a mistake to be corrected.

### 1.4 "Our competitor's site does X"

**Usually means:** "I am nervous about looking worse than them", or occasionally something
genuinely useful — they have noticed a real pattern in the market.

**Works:** take it seriously out loud, then separate the mechanism from the surface. *"Let
me look at it properly — what is it about theirs that works for you?"* Then adopt it with
credit, or: *"Theirs does that because they sell six products and need a filter. You sell one
service with a booking window, so the equivalent for you is X."* Strongest of all is naming
something you already considered: *"We looked at that — here is why we did not take it."*

**Loses:** "we shouldn't copy competitors." True, useless, and it implies they were
unsophisticated for raising it.

### 1.5 "My wife / my CEO / my nephew said…"

**Usually means:** the speaker agrees but does not want to own it, *or* someone whose opinion
genuinely matters here has seen it and you did not know.

**Works:** find out which, and take the feedback on its content, never on its source. *"What
did they say specifically?"* — then treat it exactly as the same sentence from a
professional: what is the mechanism, is it taste or business (§2). If it turns out to be a
decision-maker you did not know about, that is the most valuable thing you will learn all
meeting: *"Sounds like she has a real stake in this — should she be in the next review? It is
cheaper than relaying."*

**Loses:** any version of "they're not the target user". You may be right, and you have just
told someone that a person they care about is unqualified. The objection does not go away —
it goes underground.

### 1.6 "This looks like a template"

**Usually means:** "I cannot see what I paid for", or genuinely: it is generic. Consider that
it might be the second one before you answer.

**Works:** answer with decisions, not adjectives. *"Which part reads that way?"* Then:
*"What is specific to you here is the turnaround-time headline, the service list built around
parts pricing you cannot publish, and a booking flow that matches how your workshop actually
schedules. The parts that look familiar are familiar on purpose — a booking form that works
like every other booking form is one nobody has to learn."*

**Loses:** a defensive list of your craft decisions. Nobody was ever moved by being told the
tracking is custom.

### 1.7 "It's too different from what we have"

**Usually means:** fear of internal reaction, or of confusing existing customers. Both are
legitimate and neither is about design.

**Works:** name the fear, then de-risk it. *"Who are you worried will react badly?"* Then
offer a mechanism — a soft launch, a staged rollout, a comparison they can circulate
internally, or deliberately keeping one recognisable element: *"We can keep that exactly as
it is; it is the thing your regulars look for."*

**Loses:** "change is good", and any appeal to modernity. You are asking them to absorb a
risk you do not carry.

### 1.8 "Can we fit more above the fold?"

**Usually means:** four stakeholders each want their thing first, and you are being handed an
internal negotiation dressed as a layout note.

**Works:** make the tradeoff visible and hand the decision back. *"We can fit anything above
the fold — what comes out? Right now that space has one job, which is getting a booking
started. If the services list goes up there, the booking button goes below it. Which one do
you want a visitor to do first?"* Forcing the ranking is the entire move, and it usually
resolves the internal negotiation in the room without you having to arbitrate it.

Useful supporting fact, if you have it: scroll depth data. Most audiences scroll; the fold is
rarely the cliff people believe it is. Do not lead with the data — lead with the question,
and use the data if they push.

### 1.9 "Why did this take so long?"

**Usually means:** "I do not know what happened during those weeks", which is a
communication failure rather than a speed one. Occasionally: it genuinely did take too long.

**Works:** answer in outcomes, not activity. *"The build was three weeks; the rest was the
booking logic — your workshop schedules by bench, not by day, and no off-the-shelf tool does
that. That is where the time went, and it is why the form works the way your shop does."* If
it genuinely did take too long, say so plainly and say what you changed.

**Loses:** a list of tasks. It sounds like an invoice being itemised, which invites the room
to start questioning line items.

### 1.10 "The last agency did it for half this"

**Usually means:** a negotiating position, or a real budget problem they are embarrassed
about, or a genuine belief that these are the same product.

**Works:** do not compete on price; compete on what is included, specifically and calmly.
*"They might well have. What you are getting here is the system, so the next change is hours
rather than weeks; the accessibility work; and a performance budget that holds after we
leave. If budget is the constraint, tell me the number and I will tell you honestly what fits
— I would rather scope it down than do a worse version of all of it."*

**Loses:** disparaging the other agency, or apologising for your rate. The first is
unprofessional; the second concedes the premise.

### 1.11 Silence

**Usually means:** thinking, confused, deferring to someone who has not spoken, or a video
call where nobody wants to go first. It very rarely means agreement.

**Works:** wait longer than is comfortable — a full five seconds — then ask one *named
person* a *narrow* question. *"Priya, does the booking flow work from your side
technically?"* · *"On a scale of 'ship it' to 'start again', where is this for you?"* ·
*"What is the one thing you would change?"* A name plus a narrow question unlocks a silent
room; "any thoughts?" never has.

**Loses:** filling it by talking. You keep explaining, the room keeps not responding, and you
re-open a decision nobody had objected to.

## 2. Taste objection or business objection

**These need opposite responses, and mixing them up is the most common failure in a
review.**

| | Taste objection | Business objection |
|---|---|---|
| **Sounds like** | "I don't like…", "It feels…", "I'd prefer…" | "Our customers won't…", "This won't get us…", "Legal will…" |
| **Correct response** | **Accommodate**, if it costs little | **Investigate**, then answer with evidence or change the design |
| **Why** | It is their site and their brand. You cannot win a taste argument, you can only lose it slowly | If they are right, the design is wrong. If they are wrong, the belief still needs dismantling or it comes back |
| **Failure mode** | Defending taste with data. You look like you are dodging, and you spend credibility you will need later | Accommodating a business objection without testing it. You just built a design around someone's untested assumption |

**The diagnostic question works on both:** *"What makes you say that?"* — asked with genuine
curiosity, not as a challenge. A taste objection has no answer beyond restatement. A business
objection has a story attached, and the story is the thing you actually need.

**The trap runs both ways.** A taste objection is often *dressed* as a business one, because
people know "I don't like green" is weaker than "our customers hate green". Do not call that
out. Just ask what makes them say it, and if the answer is a restatement, treat it as taste
and accommodate it. Forcing someone to admit a preference is a preference wins you nothing
and costs you the room.

## 3. Restating before answering

Before you answer any objection, say it back in your own words and get confirmation.

> "So the concern is that a visitor landing on the homepage won't immediately see that you
> do same-week turnaround — is that right?"

Four things happen, and all four are working for you:

1. **You find out you were about to answer the wrong question** — which happens more often
   than anyone expects, and answering the wrong objection is heard as evasion.
2. **They feel heard**, which drops the temperature more than any argument.
3. **You buy three seconds** to think, visibly working rather than visibly stalling.
4. **You get to frame it.** A vague complaint becomes a specific, answerable statement — and
   a specific statement has an answer, where "it feels off" does not.

**Do not weaponise it.** Restating in a way that shrinks the objection ("so you're saying you
just don't like orange?") is transparent and it is the fastest way to make an ally into an
opponent. Restate it at full strength, at its most reasonable, and then answer that.

## 4. Disagreeing and keeping the relationship

| Rule | In practice |
|---|---|
| **Agree with the goal, disagree with the mechanism** | "You're right that people need to see the services immediately. Where I'd push back is on the list — I think it does that job worse than the summary line does" |
| **Never disagree with the feeling, only with the cause** | You cannot tell someone they do not feel something. You can absolutely offer a different reason for the feeling |
| **Put the risk in their hands, once** | For a lawful choice that only costs them: "I'd recommend against it, here's the specific risk, but it's your site and I'll build it." An accessibility failure or anything deceptive is not this row: §5 |
| **Disagree on one thing at a time** | Two simultaneous disagreements read as resistance; one reads as expertise |
| **Never disagree with a room** | If three people agree with each other and not with you, you have lost this meeting. Take it away: "let me sit with that and come back with two options" |
| **Write it down afterwards** | Anything you recommended against and built anyway goes in `MEETING_RECORD.md` §2, neutrally. Not to be right later — so the reason is recoverable when it comes up |

**The sentence that ends most disagreements productively:** *"Can we try it and look at it
next to this one?"* Most design disagreements are unresolvable in the abstract and trivially
resolvable side by side, and a client who sees both usually picks correctly. It also costs
you nothing to be wrong, because you offered the comparison.

## 5. Conceding well

**Concede the small ones immediately and visibly. It is what buys you the big ones.**

The mechanism is not manipulation, it is information. A room forms a model of you inside the
first two objections: *does this person push back on everything, or only on things that
matter?* If the answer is "everything", your objections carry no information and all of them
get discounted. If the answer is "only on things that matter", the one time you say "this one
I would push back on" carries enormous weight — precisely because of the eight times you did
not.

| Concede immediately | Hold the line |
|---|---|
| Anything parameterised — colour, photo, wording, order of two blocks | Anything that breaks accessibility, or deceives |
| Anything where you have no real argument (say so: "no strong view, happy either way") | Anything that breaks the thing the page exists to do |
| Anything that costs under an hour and has no downstream effect | Anything that creates a maintenance burden the client will inherit unknowingly |
| Anything about their business you cannot know | Anything you would be embarrassed to have your name on |

**Concede cleanly.** "Yes, that's a better idea" — full stop. A grudging concession
("well, if that's what you want…") gets you the change *and* the resentment. And when you
are holding the line, say which one you are doing, out loud: *"Most of what you have said
today I agree with. This one I want to push back on, and it is the only one."*

**A risky request has one rule**, the same in evidence.md §7.3 and in landing-page-conversion:

| The request | What you do |
|---|---|
| Lawful, and it only costs them: a weaker headline, a crowded hero, a conversion risk | Say the cost once, write it down, and build it. It is their site |
| It breaks accessibility: contrast below AA, a tap target under `--tap-min`, a keyboard trap, motion with no reduced version | Hold the line. Build the version that meets their goal and the standard, and put the risk of the other in writing. The failing version ships only on the written decision of whoever signs the site off |
| It deceives, or the law forbids it: a countdown that resets, a pre-ticked upsell, an invented review, hidden renewal terms | Decline it in one sentence and offer the honest version (landing-page-conversion's table of declined patterns) |
| "Is this allowed?" | Name the rule you know, and send the question to their lawyer. Never sign off on legality |

## 6. Being wrong in the room

It will happen: a number you got wrong, a decision built on a misunderstanding, a page you
did not know existed.

**The move is short, factual, and immediate.**

> "You're right. I had that wrong. Let me find out and come back to you by end of day."

Then actually come back, when you said, even if the answer is "still looking".

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Say it plainly and move on | Explain at length how the misunderstanding arose |
| Own it in the first person, even if it was someone else's error | Distribute blame in the room, ever |
| Give a specific time you will return with the answer | Say "I'll look into it" with no time attached |
| Ask what else was built on that assumption | Assume it was contained |
| Carry on presenting with the same confidence | Become visibly deflated for the rest of the meeting |

**The mechanism:** being wrong costs you almost nothing if you handle it in ten seconds. It
costs you the room if you defend it for two minutes. Every person in that room has been
wrong in a meeting; what they are actually watching is what you do next, because that is the
thing that predicts what you will do when something goes wrong on their project.

## 7. The decision-maker who is not there

The most expensive structural problem in a design review, and it is a scheduling problem
wearing a feedback problem's clothes. Feedback arrives second-hand, without the constraints
that produced the decisions, and you cannot ask it a question.

**Before the meeting:**

- Ask who signs off. Ask it directly, in the kickoff, and write the answer down.
- If that person is not in the review, say so early and cheerfully: *"Should Dana be on this
  one? It is usually cheaper than relaying."*
- If they genuinely cannot attend, **record a five-minute walkthrough** and send it with the
  deck. Relayed decisions survive; relayed *reasoning* does not, and it is the reasoning that
  stops a decision being reopened.

**When their feedback arrives second-hand:**

| Move | Wording |
|---|---|
| Get the actual words | "Can you send me exactly what she said? I want to make sure I am solving the right thing" |
| Find the constraint behind it | "Do you know what is behind it — has something changed on your side?" |
| Offer direct contact, lightly | "Happy to jump on fifteen minutes with her if that is easier than relaying" |
| Re-present, do not just patch | If the absent decision-maker's feedback overturns a decision, the whole decision goes back through constraint → options → choice, with them |
| Protect your ally | The person relaying is not your opponent. They are usually as frustrated as you are |

**Do not implement second-hand feedback silently.** Two rounds of that and the design is a
compromise nobody chose, assembled from fragments of a conversation you were never in.

## 8. Feedback that is genuinely incoherent

"Make it pop but keep it minimal." "More professional but more fun." Two stakeholders
asking for opposite things in the same email.

**First, assume it is coherent and you are missing the frame.** Most contradictory feedback
resolves once the unstated goal is found: "professional but fun" usually means "credible
enough to be trusted with money, human enough not to feel like a bank" — a buildable brief.

**The moves, in order:**

1. **Ask for an example.** *"Is there a site that feels the way you are describing?"* People
   are far better at recognising than at specifying, and one example resolves more than an
   hour of adjectives.
2. **Ask what it should make someone feel or do.** Reframing from attribute to outcome
   dissolves most contradictions, because the contradiction usually lives in the vocabulary
   rather than in the goal.
3. **Offer a forced choice.** *"If you had to pick one: would you rather a visitor thought
   'these people are serious' or 'these people are easy to deal with'?"* Nobody wants to
   choose and everybody can.
4. **Name the contradiction, neutrally, once.** *"I want to flag a tension — more above the
   fold and more breathing room pull against each other. Which matters more to you?"*
   Neutrally is the operative word: it is a tension in the requirements, not an error by a
   person.
5. **If two people in the room want opposite things, stop.** This is not yours to resolve.
   *"I think there are two views here and I do not want to pick one for you. Can you two
   align and come back to me? Here is what each direction would cost."* Then leave. A
   designer who arbitrates an internal political fight loses whichever side does not win.

**When it is genuinely incoherent and nobody will choose**, build the version you can defend,
document the tension in `MEETING_RECORD.md` §3, and present it as a decision with a named
alternative. Sometimes a design is how an organisation discovers it has not decided
something.

## 9. Presenting alongside more senior designers

Your specific situation: a room with senior designers whose taste is not in question, and
their clients. Three things matter and none of them is being right.

### 9.1 What you are actually being assessed on

Not whether the work is the best in the room. **Whether you can be put in front of a
client.** That is a judgement about temperament under questioning far more than about craft:
can you hold a position without being defensive, concede without collapsing, and say "I do
not know" without losing the room. Every senior in that room has watched a talented junior
lose a client meeting, and they are checking for the failure modes they have seen before.

### 9.2 When to defer, and how

Defer on: relationship history you do not have · commercial context you are not privy to ·
their studio's conventions · anything where being right is worth less than the client
relationship.

Hold on: the decisions you made, with their reasoning, until you are given a better reason.
"Because I prefer it" is not a better reason from anyone, at any seniority — but neither is
it your job to say so out loud in front of a client.

**Deferring well sounds like:** *"You know this client far better than I do — how do you want
to handle that one?"* That is not weakness. It is an accurate allocation of the thing each
of you actually knows, and seniors notice it.

**Deferring badly sounds like:** silence, then quietly doing it their way, then resenting it.
Or abandoning a decision the moment it is questioned, which tells the room the reasoning was
decoration.

### 9.3 Disagreeing with a creative director without it being a challenge

The distinction that matters: **disagree about the work, never about the authority.** A CD
overruled in front of a client has a problem with their client, not with you, and you have
just created it.

| Move | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Ask, do not assert.** "Would it be worse if we kept the list ordered by turnaround rather than alphabetically?" | An open question invites reasoning. An assertion invites a ruling, and a ruling ends the discussion |
| **Give them the constraint, let them reach the conclusion** | "The thing I keep coming back to is that 68% of these are on a phone" — a good CD will do the rest, and it is now their idea |
| **Take it offline if the room is watching** | "Can I grab you after this?" — genuinely the strongest move available. It protects them publicly and gets you a real conversation privately |
| **Separate the decision from the defence** | "Happy to change it. I want to make sure I understand the reasoning first, because I will have to defend it next week" — legitimate, deferential, and it extracts the actual reason |
| **Concede publicly, argue privately** | In front of a client, the studio has one position. Work out what it is beforehand; if it comes up live, defer, then resolve it after |
| **Never invoke a rule to win** | "That breaks the design system" in front of a CD is a junior citing process at someone whose job is judgement. Cite the consequence instead |

**The pre-meeting is the real fix.** Ninety percent of the disagreements that damage you in a
client room are disagreements you could have had in ten minutes beforehand. Send the deck
early. Ask: *"anything in here you would present differently?"* You will get the
disagreements in private, where they cost nothing and where being persuaded is free — and you
will walk into the room with one position instead of two.

## 10. The client who rejects the whole direction

Not an objection to a decision: "this is not it", said about everything, usually in the first
five minutes. The two mistakes are to defend decision by decision (each defence confirms the
verdict) and to concede everything on the spot (you now have no direction and no brief).

**Stop presenting.** The deck's remaining slides are answers to questions nobody is asking any
more. Say so: *"Then the rest of this deck is the wrong meeting. Let's use the time to find out
what changed."*

**Find out whether the brief moved.** A whole-direction rejection is nearly always one of
three things, and they need different responses:

| What happened | How you can tell | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The brief changed and nobody told you | Their objections name things the brief never asked for | Re-brief, in writing, before anything is redrawn. Scope and price move with it: `MEETING_RECORD.md` |
| A decision-maker you never met has spoken | "We showed it to…" | §7. Get the actual words; offer the fifteen minutes; re-present to them |
| It is the direction, honestly | They can say what they expected and it is inside the brief | Go back to the decision log: which constraint did you read wrongly? That is a rework you owe, and it is bounded by the log |

**Do not redraw in the room.** "What if we…" sketched live is a third direction chosen under
pressure. Take the constraints you have just learned, write them into the log as a reversed
decision with its reason (the deck shows it as reversed, never as the current call), and
come back with the options.

**The record.** The same-day record (`assets/MEETING_RECORD.md`) matters more here than
after any other meeting: the sentence to get confirmed is *what they expected instead*, in
their words, because next round is judged against it.
